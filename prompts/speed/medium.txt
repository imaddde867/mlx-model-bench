At the northern edge of the bay stands a lighthouse that has not been lit for forty years. Its stone has weathered to the colour of old paper, and the iron gallery has rusted into a lace of holes. Sailors still use it as a landmark, though they no longer depend on it for warning. On clear evenings the tower throws a long shadow across the water, and the shadow moves with the tide in a way that seems almost deliberate.

The keeper's cottage beside it was built from the same grey stone, with a slate roof and windows set deep into the walls. Inside, the rooms are small and low, and the floors slope gently toward the sea. A stove sits in the corner of the kitchen, cold now, its chimney capped against the weather. Someone has left a tin of tea on the shelf above the sink, and the label has faded to a pale yellow.

Maps of the coast were once drawn by hand, one line at a time, by people who walked the shore with a notebook and a length of rope. They measured distance by pacing, recorded the height of cliffs by dropping stones, and noted the colour of the water in the margin. Their maps were imperfect, but they carried a kind of attention that a satellite image cannot reproduce: every inlet had a name, and every name had a story attached to it.

Measuring anything well requires patience. The instrument must be checked, the conditions recorded, and the observer must resist the temptation to round the result toward the answer they expect. A single reading is rarely enough; repetition reveals the spread, and the spread is often more informative than the average. This is true of tide tables, of temperature records, and of the quiet work of comparing one machine against another.

By the middle of the century the harbour had silted up, and the fishing boats moved east to a deeper anchorage. The cottages along the lane were sold and repainted, and the lane itself was widened to take cars. Only the tower kept its original shape, because there was no reason to change it and no money to take it down. Visitors came in summer to photograph it against the sky, and left again before the weather turned.

Nothing about the tower is exceptional. It is not the tallest or the oldest or the most beautiful on this coast. It is simply there, doing the small work of standing still while everything around it changes. That may be the most useful thing a landmark can do: to remain in place long enough that other people can measure how far they have travelled from it.
